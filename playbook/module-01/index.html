Step 0. Before you read anything else: fix this

Don’t read ahead. Seriously. This module starts with a little trap, and it only works if you walk into it.

Pick one bug card below. Choose whichever feels most like your world. Then fix the bug any way you like. Note the time when you start. Come back to the line that says “STOP HERE” when it’s fixed.

Card D: the developer card (Java 11 or newer)

Save this as TuneUp.java and run java TuneUp.java:

// Example Cycle Works (FICTIONAL TEST BUSINESS)
// Rule: riders who log 10 or more rides get a free tune-up.
public class TuneUp {

    static boolean isFreeTuneUpEligible(int ridesLogged) {
        return ridesLogged > 10;
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) {
        check(!isFreeTuneUpEligible(9), "9 rides: not eligible");
        check(isFreeTuneUpEligible(10), "10 rides: eligible");
        check(isFreeTuneUpEligible(25), "25 rides: eligible");
        System.out.println("All checks passed");
    }

    static void check(boolean ok, String name) {
        if (!ok) throw new AssertionError("FAILED: " + name);
        System.out.println("ok - " + name);
    }
}

The problem: it stops with FAILED: 10 rides: eligible. Fix it.

Go on. Fix your card. We’ll wait.

✋ STOP HERE (only once your bug is fixed)

Now answer one question honestly. Nobody else will see your answer.

How many lines did you type by hand into the card itself (the cell, the flyer, the HTML, or the Java file) before you asked an AI to do it?

Count a line for every cell, sentence, or line of code where you typed, deleted, or pasted your own characters. Typing in an AI’s chat box doesn’t count. Reading and looking around doesn’t count either. Looking is good.

Write the number down. That’s your Hands score for round one. In this playbook, the target is zero.

If you got zero on the first try: lovely, you’re ahead. If you got 1, 3, or 12: welcome to the club. Most of us have spent years being rewarded for fixing things ourselves, and the reflex is fast. It reaches for the keyboard before the thinking part of you has finished its coffee. That reflex is the first thing this playbook asks you to unlearn.

A tale of two fixers (characters are fictional)

Picture two people with the same broken garden spreadsheet.

Ray has written Java for thirty years. He spots the problem in four seconds: the formula stops at row 9. He clicks the cell, types B11, and moves on. Total: 32. Done, he thinks. He doesn’t notice “3 hrs” sitting in Maple’s row as text, which the formula quietly skips. The real answer is 35.

Dee spent the last eight years running a busy household, the school fundraiser, and a carpool schedule with more moving parts than most software. She’s coming back to work and doesn’t know spreadsheet formulas well. So she does what she has done a thousand times with a teenager, a contractor, or a committee: she explains the job clearly. She says what the total should be, where the numbers are, what not to touch, and how she’ll know it’s right. The AI fixes both problems. Then Dee asks it to list what it changed, checks the total against the paper log, and writes one line in her notebook.

Ray is the better programmer. Dee did the better job. She didn’t type a single character into the spreadsheet, and she knows exactly what changed and why.

That’s the whole playbook in one story. The rest is practice.

Fair to Ray: his 30 years aren’t wasted. They’re a huge advantage once he points them at the right job. Knowing what “correct” looks like, spotting edge cases, smelling a sneaky change: that’s exactly what an AI’s work needs. He just has to stop spending that expertise on typing.

Key idea 1: Unlearning is the core skill

The tools change every few months. The mindset shift is what lasts. Here are the three big unlearnings:

The old reflex and the new one
The old reflexThe new reflexWhat it sounds like
“It’s faster if I just do it.” “It’s faster if I say it well once.” You describe the fix, then review it. Your hands stay off the work itself
“My value is knowing how.” “My value is knowing what good looks like, and why.” You think like a product owner: the goal, the user, the rules, the trade-offs
“It’s done when it looks right.” “It’s done when it meets the done criteria I wrote first.” You decide how you’ll check before the work starts, not after

And one more, for anyone who has ever said “I’m not technical”:

For anyone who has said they are not technical
The old reflexThe new reflexWhat it sounds like
“This isn’t for people like me.” “If I can explain it to a person, I can explain it to an agent.” Your experience organizing people, projects, and households counts here

A quick word on “agent.” An AI agent is an AI that doesn’t just chat. It can take steps toward a goal: use tools, edit files, look things up, and check its own work, often in a loop. The more an AI can do, the more your instructions matter, because it acts on them.

Who has a head start?

Anyone who has gotten things done through other people:

  • the parent who runs a household on sticky notes and group chats;
  • the nurse who coordinates a shift handoff;
  • the teacher who writes instructions for a substitute;
  • the shop owner who trains a new hire on a Saturday;
  • the volunteer who organizes forty people with one email.

That’s delegation, and it’s a skill. You’ve been practicing it for years.

Developers have a different head start: you already know how to define “correct” (tests, edge cases, reviews). The unlearning for you is letting go of the keyboard.

Key idea 2: Communication is the fundamental skill

You can’t get anything done with an agent unless you can get your thinking out of your head and into words. An agent can’t read your mind, and it doesn’t know what you know. It will happily fill every gap with a guess, and often a confident one.

That’s why the most common frustration isn’t “AI is useless.” It’s “AI is almost right.” In the 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, 66% of developers named “AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite” as a frustration, and more developers said they distrust the accuracy of AI tools (46%) than trust it (33%) (survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai). “Almost right” is very often a communication problem: something the AI needed to know never got said.

The Brief Card: five lines that do the heavy lifting

Every good request to an agent answers five questions. Write them on an index card, a sticky note, or the top of your chat.

The five lines of a Brief Card, with the garden spreadsheet as the example
LineAsk yourselfGarden spreadsheet example
1. IntentWhat do I want, and why?“The Total should show the season’s volunteer hours.”
2. ContextWhat does the agent need to know that it can’t see?“Hours are in column B, rows 2–11. The paper log says 35.”
3. ConstraintsWhat must it not do? What are the rules?“Don’t rename or delete anyone. Keep hours as plain numbers.”
4. Done whenHow will I check that it worked?“B13 shows 35, and still adds up if I add a volunteer in row 12.”
5. ExamplesWhat does good look like?“‘3 hrs’ should count as 3.”

Memory trick: “I Can Count Done Examples.” Intent, Context, Constraints, Done when, Examples.

Not every request needs all five at full length. “Make this email friendlier” is fine on its own. But when something matters, or the first answer came back “almost right,” the Brief Card is how you fix the request instead of fixing the work.

Worked examples: a bad request vs. a good one

For non-coders: the garden spreadsheet (Card A)

❌ The “almost right” request

fix my spreadsheet the total is wrong

What usually happens: the AI guesses. It might extend the range to row 11 and give you 32. That’s closer, but still wrong, because it never learned that “3 hrs” is text, or that you expect 35. You’d be tempted to click in and fix it yourself. Trap!

✅ The Brief Card request

Intent: The Total cell (B13) should show the total volunteer hours for the fall season.
Context: Hours are in column B, rows 2 to 11 (ten volunteers). The paper log says the total is 35. One entry, Maple's, was typed as "3 hrs."
Constraints: Don't rename or delete anyone. Keep every hour as a plain number. Don't change the layout.
Done when: B13 shows 35, and the total still includes a new volunteer if I add one in row 12.
Example: "3 hrs" should become 3.
Before you change anything, tell me in one sentence what you think is wrong. After, list every cell you changed.

Notice the last two lines. They’re your review checkpoints (more in Key idea 3). If your AI assistant can’t edit the sheet directly, it will give you the corrected formula and value. Paste them in exactly as given. That counts as zero, because you’re applying the agent’s work, not doing your own.

For developers: the tune-up rule (Card D)

❌ The “almost right” request

TuneUp failing, make the tests pass

This is the most dangerous sentence in AI-assisted coding. “Make the tests pass” can be satisfied by changing the test. An agent that deletes the 10 rides check has, technically, done what you asked.

✅ The Brief Card request

Intent: Riders with 10 or more logged rides get a free tune-up. isFreeTuneUpEligible must match that business rule.
Context: File TuneUp.java. The check "10 rides: eligible" fails. Run it with java TuneUp.java.
Constraints: Change only the body of isFreeTuneUpEligible. Do not edit, remove, or weaken any check(...) line. No new files or dependencies. Negative ride counts can't happen upstream, so don't add handling for them.
Done when: java TuneUp.java prints "All checks passed," and you show me the diff (I expect about one line).
Examples: 9 → false, 10 → true, 25 → true.
First tell me the root cause in one sentence; then make the change.

What you should see: a one-character diff (> becomes >=) and an explanation like “the comparison excluded exactly 10.” If the agent proposes anything bigger, that’s your cue to ask why. Don’t grab the keyboard.

See the tool, then learn the craft

Here’s something that surprises people: a customer-help bot’s instructions are just a brief. The bot reads it before every single conversation. A vague brief doesn’t just give you one “almost right” answer. It gives every customer one.

Key idea 3: Stay the owner (delegate the typing, not the responsibility)

Here’s the risk of getting good at delegating: you stop knowing what’s going on. The agent did “something,” the number looks right, and you move on. Three weeks later someone asks, “Why does this formula look like that?” and you have no idea.

The owner is the person who can answer for the work, whoever did the typing. Three habits keep you the owner:

1. Two checkpoints in every request.

  • Before: “Tell me what you think is wrong and what you plan to change.” You catch misunderstandings while they’re still cheap.
  • After: “List every change you made, and why.” You catch the sneaky extras.

Then check it yourself against your Done-when line. Don’t take the agent’s word that it worked.

2. The Explain-It-Back test. Without looking, explain the fix out loud in two sentences: what was wrong, what changed, and how you know it’s fixed. For Card A: “The total skipped the last two rows and ignored ‘3 hrs’ because it was text. I had the agent extend the range and convert it to 3, and the total now matches the paper log at 35.” If you can’t do it, you don’t own the fix yet. Ask the agent to explain, then try again. That’s allowed; it’s how you learn.

3. The Ownership Log (your habit for this module). After every delegation that matters, write one line:

Example log line for the fictional garden spreadsheet
Date I asked for… What changed (and why) How I checked Would I undo it?
Oct 3 Fix garden total Range B2:B9 → B2:B12; Maple “3 hrs” → 3 (text was skipped) Matches paper log, 35; added a test row, still adds up No

That takes 30 seconds. Over time it becomes your memory of the project. Later in the playbook it turns into your “what broke” write-up, which is the thing hiring managers ask about most.

When to take the keyboard back:

  • Real private information. Don’t paste real names, health details, account numbers, or passwords into any AI tool. These cards are fictional for exactly that reason.
  • Anything that can’t be undone, like sending, deleting, or paying. You press that button yourself, after you’ve checked.
  • When it’s genuinely faster to fix one character than to explain it. That’s fine in real life. The zero-hands rule is a training drill that builds the “describe first” reflex. It isn’t a rule for every minute of your working life.

How to score yourself

Hands = lines you typed, deleted, or pasted by hand into the card itself.

  • Counts: any cell, sentence line, or code line where you put your own characters (including deleting, and including undo).
  • Doesn’t count:
    • typing in the AI’s chat box;
    • pasting the agent’s output unchanged into the card (if your assistant can’t edit it directly);
    • setup, like opening or copying the original card;
    • reading, clicking around, and inspecting. Look all you want.
  • Not sure? Count it.

Two special results:

  • 🙈 Eyes Closed: Hands = 0 but you skipped checking. That doesn’t count as a win. Zero typing without checking isn’t ownership; it’s hoping.
  • 🔎 Caught It: the agent came back “almost right” and you caught it before accepting. No points, just bragging rights. This is the most valuable moment in the whole game.

Honor rules:

  • This is self-reported, and nothing is sent anywhere.
  • An honest “3” teaches you more than a fake “0,” because the whole point is noticing your own reflex. When you catch yourself reaching for the keyboard, write TRAP in your log and smile. That’s the lesson landing.
  • If you want proof for yourself, screen-record your attempt and keep it private.

No AI? Play the paper version: “Human Agent”

Grab a friend, partner, or teenager. They play the agent, and they must follow your written brief exactly, literally, and nothing more.

  • Round 1: they can’t ask questions (just like an agent that guesses).
  • Round 2: they may ask one question.

Try it on Card B with a pen and paper, or on something silly like “make me a sandwich.” You’ll learn more about briefs in five minutes of laughing than in an hour of reading. Score it the same way: Hands is the number of times you grabbed the pen.

Two players can use the same rank and Owner Score above. Nothing about the other person is stored. If you share a browser, use Clear my data between turns.
